Rules 1–3: Basic Agreement

(•‿•)
Artie is loading...

Rule 1: Singular Subject → Singular Verb

A singular subject takes a singular verb form.

  • The dog runs fast.
  • She writes beautiful poems.
  • The book is on the table.

Rule 2: Plural Subject → Plural Verb

A plural subject takes a plural verb form.

  • The dogs run fast.
  • They write beautiful poems.
  • The books are on the table.

Rule 3: Compound Subjects with "and"

Two or more subjects joined by "and" take a plural verb.

  • Ram and Shyam are friends.
  • The teacher and the principal have arrived.
EXCEPTION If two nouns joined by "and" refer to the same person or thing, use a singular verb:
  • Bread and butter is his breakfast. (one combined item)
  • The actor and director has arrived. (same person)

Rules 4–5: Either/Or & Intervening Phrases

(•‿•)
Artie is loading...

Rule 4: Either/Or, Neither/Nor — Proximity Rule

When subjects are joined by either...or, neither...nor, or, or nor, the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it.

  • Neither the teacher nor the students were present. (students = nearest, plural)
  • Neither the students nor the teacher was present. (teacher = nearest, singular)
  • Either you or he is responsible.

Rule 5: Intervening Phrases Don't Affect Agreement

Phrases like along with, as well as, together with, in addition to, accompanied by do NOT make the subject plural. The verb agrees with the original subject only.

  • The captain, along with his team, is ready. (subject = captain, singular)
  • Rina, as well as her friends, has gone to the market.
  • The teacher, together with the students, was present.
ICSE TRAP This is the #1 trick in ICSE exams! Students confuse "along with" for "and". Remember: "along with" ≠ "and". Only "and" makes a compound subject.

Rules 6–8: Collective Nouns & Pronouns

(•‿•)
Artie is loading...

Rule 6: Collective Nouns

Collective nouns (team, committee, family, audience, jury) take a singular verb when acting as one unit, and a plural verb when members act individually.

  • The team is playing well. (acting as one unit)
  • The team are arguing among themselves. (members acting individually)
  • The jury has given its verdict. (unified decision)

Rule 7: Each, Every, Everyone, Everybody → Singular

These words are always singular, even when followed by plural nouns.

  • Each of the students has submitted the assignment.
  • Every boy and girl was given a prize.
  • Everyone is expected to attend.
  • Everybody likes ice cream.

Rule 8: Some, All, Most, None — Depends on the Noun

These words can be singular or plural depending on whether the noun they refer to is countable or uncountable.

Pronoun + Noun Type Verb Example
Some + uncountable Singular Some of the water is dirty.
Some + countable plural Plural Some of the apples are rotten.
All + uncountable Singular All the furniture has been sold.
All + countable plural Plural All the chairs have been sold.

Rules 9–11: Special Nouns & Titles

(•‿•)

Rule 9: Uncountable Nouns That Look Plural → Singular

Some nouns end in -s but are actually singular:

  • Mathematics is my favourite subject.
  • The news was shocking.
  • Physics requires logical thinking.
  • Measles is a contagious disease.

Rule 10: "The number of" vs "A number of"

Phrase Meaning Verb Example
The number of A specific quantity Singular The number of students is increasing.
A number of Many / several Plural A number of students are absent.

Rule 11: Titles, Book Names, Country Names → Singular

Even when they contain plural words, titles and names are treated as singular.

  • "The Arabian Nights" is an interesting book.
  • The United States is a large country.
  • "Gulliver's Travels" was written by Jonathan Swift.

Rules 12–15: Advanced Rules

(•‿•)
Artie is loading...

Rule 12: Distances, Amounts, Time Periods → Singular

When a distance, sum of money, or time period is considered as a single unit, use a singular verb.

  • Ten kilometres is a long distance.
  • Five hundred rupees is a reasonable price.
  • Three hours is enough time to finish.

Rule 13: "There" + Be → Agrees with Real Subject

In sentences starting with there, the verb agrees with the noun that follows it (the real subject).

  • There is a book on the table. (book = singular)
  • There are many books on the shelf. (books = plural)
  • There was no reason to worry.

Rule 14: Inverted Sentences

In questions and sentences beginning with here/there, identify the real subject (which comes after the verb).

  • Here comes the bus. (bus = singular)
  • Where are the keys? (keys = plural)

Rule 15: Relative Pronouns (Who, Which, That)

A relative pronoun takes a verb that agrees with its antecedent (the word it refers to).

  • He is one of the students who are hardworking. (who = students, plural)
  • She is the only one of the girls who has qualified. (who = the only one, singular)
  • It is I who am responsible. (who = I)
EXAM TIP "One of the ... who" is a classic ICSE trap. Ask yourself: does "who" refer to "one" or to the plural noun? Usually it refers to the plural noun ("the students"), so the verb is plural.
REF_ID: 207_BONUS_RULES

Bonus Rules & Common Traps

(•‿•)
Artie is loading...

Nouns with Two Parts → Plural Verb

Nouns for things with two parts always take a plural verb:

  • The scissors are on the table.
  • My trousers are torn.
  • These glasses belong to my father.

But with "a pair of", use a singular verb:

  • A pair of scissors is needed.

Doesn't vs Don't

Form Used With Example
Doesn't Singular (he, she, it) He doesn't like coffee.
Don't Plural (I, we, they, you) They don't like coffee.

"One of the ..." → Singular Verb

When "one of the" precedes a plural noun, the verb is singular because the subject is "one".

  • One of the students is absent.
  • One of these books belongs to me.
