Laws of Refraction & Snell's Law

(•‿•)
Loading Phys-Bot...

1. What is Refraction?

Refraction is the change in direction of a ray of light as it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another. It occurs because the speed of light changes when entering a different medium.

  • Denser → Rarer: Light bends away from the normal (angle of refraction > angle of incidence). Speed increases.
  • Rarer → Denser: Light bends towards the normal (angle of refraction < angle of incidence). Speed decreases.
  • Normal incidence (i = 0°): Light passes straight through without bending, but speed still changes.

2. Laws of Refraction

  1. First Law: The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. Second Law (Snell's Law): The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for a given pair of media and for light of a given wavelength:
    sin i / sin r = constant = 1μ2 (refractive index of medium 2 w.r.t. medium 1)

3. Refractive Index

  • Absolute Refractive Index (μ): Ratio of speed of light in vacuum ($c$) to speed of light in the medium ($v$):
    μ = c / v Since $c > v$ for all media, $\mu$ is always greater than 1. A higher $\mu$ means a denser (slower) medium.
  • Relative Refractive Index (1μ2): Refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1:
    1μ2 = μ2 / μ1 = v1 / v2
  • Reciprocal Relation:
    1μ2 = 1 / 2μ1

1. Refraction Through a Rectangular Glass Slab

When a ray of light passes through a rectangular glass slab with parallel faces:

  • At the first face (air → glass): Ray bends towards the normal (enters denser medium).
  • At the second face (glass → air): Ray bends away from the normal (enters rarer medium) by exactly the same amount.
  • The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced (shifted sideways). The angle of emergence equals the angle of incidence ($e = i$).

2. Lateral Displacement

The perpendicular distance between the incident ray produced forward and the emergent ray is called the lateral displacement (d).

Lateral displacement increases when:

  1. The thickness of the glass slab increases.
  2. The angle of incidence increases (from 0° towards 90°).
  3. The refractive index of the slab material increases (denser glass causes more bending).

At normal incidence ($i = 0°$): lateral displacement is zero (ray passes straight through undeviated).

3. Real Depth & Apparent Depth

When viewing an object placed in a denser medium (like a coin at the bottom of a pool) from a rarer medium (like air):

  • The object appears to be raised up (closer to the surface) than it actually is.
  • Apparent Depth < Real Depth (object appears shallower).

μ = Real Depth / Apparent Depth

Shift (Rise) = Real Depth − Apparent Depth = Real Depth × (1 − 1/μ)

4. Applications & Examples

  • A swimming pool appears shallower than its actual depth.
  • A coin at the bottom of a glass of water appears raised.
  • A stick partially immersed in water appears bent at the water surface.
  • Stars twinkle due to atmospheric refraction (varying density layers).
  • Sun appears to rise 2 minutes early and set 2 minutes late due to atmospheric refraction.

Critical Angle & Total Internal Reflection

1. Critical Angle (ic)

The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is exactly 90° (the refracted ray grazes along the surface).

μ = 1 / sin ic  ⟹  sin ic = 1 / μ

A medium with a higher refractive index (denser) has a smaller critical angle.

Medium (w.r.t. air)μCritical Angle (ic)
Water1.3348.75°
Crown Glass1.5241.14°
Diamond2.4224.41°

2. Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

When light travels from a denser to a rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, the light is completely reflected back into the denser medium. No refraction occurs — the surface acts as a perfect mirror.

Two Essential Conditions for TIR:

  1. Light must travel from a denser medium to a rarer medium (e.g., glass → air, water → air).
  2. The angle of incidence in the denser medium must be greater than the critical angle ($i > i_c$).

3. Applications of TIR

  • Optical Fibres: Light enters a thin glass fibre at one end and undergoes TIR repeatedly at the walls, travelling long distances without signal loss. Used in telecommunications, endoscopy, and internet cables.
  • Brilliance of Diamond: Diamond has a very small critical angle (24.41°). Most light entering the diamond undergoes multiple TIR before emerging, producing extraordinary sparkle and fire.
  • Mirage: On hot days, air near the ground is rarer (less dense, lower μ) than the cooler air above. Light from the sky undergoes progressive refraction through these layers and eventually TIR, creating an illusion of water on the road.
  • Totally Reflecting Prisms (45°–90°–45°): Used in periscopes, binoculars, and SLR cameras instead of plane mirrors (produce brighter, sharper reflections with zero light absorption).
