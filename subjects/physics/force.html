Moment of a Force (Torque)

(•‿•)
Loading Phys-Bot...

1. Turning Effect of Force

When a force acts on a body pivoted at a point, it turns or rotates the body about that pivot (axis of rotation). This turning effect is called the Moment of Force or Torque (τ).

2. Mathematical Expression

The moment of force depends on two factors:

  • The magnitude of the force applied ($F$).
  • The perpendicular distance of the line of action of force from the pivot ($d$).

Moment of Force (τ) = Force (F) × Perpendicular Distance (d)

3. Units and Conversions

SystemUnit of ForceUnit of DistanceUnit of Torque
SI SystemNewton (N)Meter (m)Newton-meter (N m)
CGS SystemDyne (dyn)Centimeter (cm)Dyne-centimeter (dyn cm)

Conversion Factors:

  • 1 N m = 105 dyn × 102 cm = 107 dyn cm
  • 1 kgf m = 9.8 N m
  • 1 gf cm = 980 dyn cm

4. Sign Convention

  • Anti-clockwise moment: Tends to turn the body anti-clockwise. Taken as positive (+).
  • Clockwise moment: Tends to turn the body clockwise. Taken as negative (-).

5. State of Equilibrium

When a number of forces acting on a body produce no change in its state of rest or of linear/rotational motion, the body is in equilibrium.

  • Static Equilibrium: Body remains at rest (e.g., book on a table, balanced scale).
  • Dynamic Equilibrium: Body remains in uniform motion (e.g., rain falling at terminal velocity, planet orbiting).
  • Conditions for Equilibrium:
    1. The algebraic sum of all forces must be zero (∑ F = 0).
    2. The algebraic sum of moments of all forces about any point must be zero (∑ τ = 0, i.e., Sum of Anti-clockwise Moments = Sum of Clockwise Moments).

Principle of Moments & Center of Gravity

(•‿•)
Loading Phys-Bot...

1. Principle of Moments

According to this principle, if a body is pivoted and is in rotational equilibrium, then:

Sum of Anti-clockwise Moments = Sum of Clockwise Moments

For a pivoted beam with masses W1 and W2 suspended at distances d1 (left) and d2 (right) from the fulcrum:

W1 × d1 = W2 × d2

2. Center of Gravity (CG)

The Center of Gravity of a body is that point through which the entire weight of the body acts, regardless of the body's orientation. The algebraic sum of moments of weights of all particles of the body about the CG is zero.

  • The CG of a body can lie within or outside its physical material (e.g., geometric center of a hollow sphere or ring contains no material, but is its CG).
  • The position of the CG depends on the shape of the body and the distribution of mass within it. If a body is deformed, its CG shifts.

1. Defining Circular Motion

When a particle moves along a circular path, its motion is called circular motion. If the particle travels with a constant speed, the motion is called Uniform Circular Motion.

2. Circular vs Linear Motion

PropertyUniform Linear MotionUniform Circular Motion
SpeedConstantConstant
VelocityConstant (direction does not change)Variable (direction changes continuously)
AccelerationZero (a = 0)Non-zero (Centripetal Acceleration acts inwards)
Net ForceZeroNon-zero (Centripetal Force acts inwards)

Note: Uniform Circular Motion is always an accelerated motion, even though the speed is constant, because the velocity vector is constantly changing direction.

3. Centripetal Force

A force acting on a body performing circular motion, directed towards the center of the circular path, is called Centripetal Force. Without it, the body would fly off tangentially due to inertia.

  • It is a real force, provided by physical mechanisms:
    • Earth orbiting Sun: Gravitational force provides centripetal force.
    • Stone whirled on a string: Tension in string provides centripetal force.
    • Car turning a corner: Friction between tyres and road provides centripetal force.

4. Centrifugal Force

A pseudo-force (fictitious force) that appears to act radially outwards on a body in a rotating (non-inertial) frame of reference is called Centrifugal Force.

  • It is not a real force; it does not exist in an inertial frame.
  • It is not the reaction force of centripetal force. Action-reaction pairs act on different bodies. Centripetal and centrifugal forces act on the same body in different reference frames, so they cannot be Newton's third law pairs.
