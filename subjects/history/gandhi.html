Chapter 8: Mahatma Gandhi & Mass Movements

Welcome, Kinjal! We are entering the most influential phase of India's struggle for freedom: the **Gandhian Era (1915–1947)**. Mahatma Gandhi transformed the national struggle into a powerful, peaceful **Mass Movement** involving millions of ordinary citizens—peasants, workers, students, and women. In this module, we will deconstruct the three major mass campaigns: the **Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22)**, the **Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34)**, and the **Quit India Movement (1942)**.

Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922)

(•‿•)
Loading History-Bot...

1. Causes and Triggers

  • Rowlatt Act (1919): Authorized the government to imprison anyone without a warrant and try them in secret without a jury, severely gagging civil liberties. Described as *"No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"*.
  • Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919): General Dyer ordered British troops to open fire on a peaceful public gathering in Amritsar, killing hundreds. This brutality completely shattered Gandhi's belief in British justice.
  • Khilafat Movement: Launched by the Ali Brothers (Mohammad and Shaukat) against the British ill-treatment of the Caliph of Turkey. Gandhi unified Hindus and Muslims by incorporating Khilafat into the Non-Cooperation demand.

2. Programme and Methods

  • Boycott: Rejection of government schools, colleges, British courts, and foreign clothes. Refusal to stand for council elections and returning British titles (e.g. Gandhi returned his *Kaisar-i-Hind* medal).
  • Constructive Programme: Promoting **Khadi** (hand-spun cloth), establishing national universities (like Jamia Millia Islamia and Kashi Vidyapeeth), and working to abolish untouchability.

3. Suspension & The Chauri Chaura Incident (1922)

In February 1922, a peaceful procession at **Chauri Chaura** (UP) turned violent. An angry mob set fire to a police station, burning **22 policemen alive**. True to his absolute belief in **Ahimsa** (non-violence), Gandhi immediately suspended the entire Non-Cooperation Movement, despite protests from other leaders.

Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934)

(•‿•)
Loading History-Bot...

1. Triggers & Road to Civil Disobedience

  • The Simon Commission (1928): A British commission sent to review constitutional reforms in India. Because it contained **zero Indian members**, it was boycotted site-wide under the slogan *"Simon Go Back"*. Lala Lajpat Rai died following a brutal lathi-charge during a Simon protest.
  • Declaration of Purna Swaraj (1929): Under the presidency of Jawaharlal Nehru at the Lahore Session, the Congress declared **Purna Swaraj** (Complete Independence) as its official goal and hoisted the tricolor on 31 December 1929.

2. The Salt Satyagraha & Dandi March (1930)

On 12 March 1930, Gandhi marched **240 miles** from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi with 78 followers. On 6 April 1930, he picked up a handful of salt, violating the British monopoly on salt production, signaling the official start of the Civil Disobedience Movement.

3. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931)

To break the political deadlock, Viceroy Irwin and Gandhi signed a pact. Gandhi agreed to **suspend the movement** and attend the **Second Round Table Conference** in London, while the British agreed to release political prisoners and allow peaceful picketing.

Quit India Movement (1942)

(•‿•)
Loading History-Bot...

1. Triggers of the Quit India Call

  • Failure of the Cripps Mission (1942): Sir Stafford Cripps offered post-war Dominion Status and a constitution-making body. Gandhi rejected this offer as a *"post-dated cheque on a failing bank"* because it did not offer immediate independence and threatened to partition India.
  • Japanese Threat: During WWII, Japanese forces advanced through Burma to India's border. Indian leaders realized that Japan was attacking India because of British presence, and only British departure could safeguard the country.

2. The "Do or Die" Call

On 8 August 1942, the Congress passed the historic **Quit India Resolution** in Bombay. Gandhi gave the nation the ultimate, uncompromising mantra: **"Do or Die"**—we shall either free India or die in the attempt.

3. British Repression and Impact

In a pre-dawn raid on 9 August 1942, British authorities arrested Gandhi and all members of the Congress Working Committee, banning the Congress. Despite the lack of senior leadership, ordinary citizens rose up in protest, launching strikes, damaging railways, and setting up parallel governments (like in Satara and Tamluk).
